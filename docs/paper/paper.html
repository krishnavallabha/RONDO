Abstract
We label every frame of a football match (25 fps, 199,319 frames) as home possession, away possession or dead ball, from automatic player and ball tracking in which the ball is missing on 41% of the graded frames. The ranking metric, spellF1, rewards whole possessions matched by temporal overlap rather than per-frame accuracy, so we separate the problem into per-frame evidence and a sequence decoder. Two learned emission models give, for every frame, the probability that the ball is dead and the probability that the home team has it; an explicit-duration hidden Markov model turns these into possessions under the challenge's rules. No label of the target match is used: every model and setting is fitted on nine labelled external matches, degraded by a simulator whose parameters are matched to label-free statistics of our match. Relabelling the training data to the challenge's notion of controlled possession, square-root possession weighting, and a gated gradient-boosted stoppage model (M8) take held-out spellF1 from 0.51 to 0.61. We also report fifteen ideas that did not pass a pre-registered keep rule. The submission is reproduced byte for byte from a fresh clone.

1  Introduction

Who has the ball is the most basic fact of a football match, yet tracking data alone does not state it. The task here is to recover it frame by frame for one La Liga match, FC Barcelona vs Athletic Club, as one of four labels: H (home possession, ball alive), A (away possession), D (dead ball) and X (outside the halves). The organisers score the labels with spellF1: predicted and true possession runs are matched by temporal intersection over union and an F1 is averaged over overlap thresholds, with per-frame macro-F1 as the tie-break.

Two properties of the data shape our approach. First, the ball is missing on 41% of graded frames, 85% of them in gaps longer than 10 s, and players move at 1.00 m/s while it is missing against 1.94 m/s while it is seen. A missing ball is therefore itself evidence, mostly of a stoppage. Second, the metric punishes fragmentation: a frame classifier that flickers creates false possessions that each cost a match. Both point to a model that reasons over time and can carry a possession across a gap.

Our contributions are: (i) a split between learned per-frame emissions and a duration-constrained decoder, with posteriors converted to scaled likelihoods (§3); (ii) a validation harness that degrades nine labelled external matches to resemble ours, including a simulator fitted to our match's label-free statistics (§4); (iii) training labels relabelled to the challenge's definition of controlled possession, and a gated stoppage model for short stoppages with the ball in view (§3.3–3.4); and (iv) a record of what did not work, each judged by a rule fixed in advance (§6).

2  Related Work

Possession and events from tracking. Link and Hoernig [1] define individual possession from ball acceleration peaks and player proximity, with team possession persisting until an opponent gains individual possession, the same idea as the challenge's controlled possession. Vidal-Codina et al. [2] detect passes, shots and restarts from tracking and classify restarts by player geometry. When the ball is unobserved, Kim et al. [3] infer its trajectory from players alone (team-possession accuracy 85% with no ball, 97% with 20% of ball samples), and TranSPORTmer [4] classifies ball states including out-of-play with 80.8% frame accuracy with the ball fully masked. Our setting sits between these: the ball is partly seen, and its absence is informative.

Temporal segmentation. In action segmentation, multi-stage temporal networks [5] and boundary-aware refinement [6] raise segmental F1 far more than frame accuracy, mostly by suppressing over-segmentation. Stacked sequential learning [7] feeds out-of-fold predictions of neighbouring frames to a second learner. We use the older, more data-efficient route of a hybrid model: discriminative frame posteriors divided by class priors as HMM emissions [8], decoded under explicit duration constraints in the manner of hidden semi-Markov models [9].

Training under shift. Our external matches have cleaner balls than the target. Domain randomisation [10] trains over a range of synthetic corruptions so that the real data looks like one more variation, and ensembles are the most robust predictors under shift [11]. We randomise corruption around values fitted to the target's label-free statistics and validate only on held-out external matches.

3  Method

3.1  Evidence

The ball track is cleaned by fixed physical rules: positions more than 10 m off the pitch and jumps faster than 40 m/s are dropped, short excursions are removed, gaps up to 0.5 s (2 s when entry and exit velocities agree) are interpolated, and each stretch is smoothed with a zero-phase 1.25 Hz filter. From the cleaned data we compute touches, sustained control, distances to the lines, ball-gap facts and player-movement statistics. Smart Tagging set-piece intervals mark restarts; the restart frame is localised from tracking, and the taking team pulls the team share over ±10 frames. Kick-off owners follow from Law 8 positions.

3.2  Emissions and decoding

The emission models output pdead(t) and phome(t), the probability that the home team has the ball given it is alive. They give a posterior over the three states,

P(H | xt) = (1 − pdead) phome,   P(A | xt) = (1 − pdead)(1 − phome),   P(D | xt) = pdead.
(1)

Because the transitions already carry the class prior, we divide it out and use scaled likelihoods as emissions [8], with π = ((1−q)/2, (1−q)/2, q) and q = 0.42, the dead share of the training matches:

log bs(xt) = log P(s | xt) − log πs.
(2)

Each state is expanded into a chain of sub-states so that a possession lasts at least dH = dA = 75 frames (3 s) and a stoppage at least dD = 25 frames (1 s); a spell cannot re-enter its own chain. Direct H↔A transitions (interceptions) are allowed but cost more than passing through D. The labels are the Viterbi path

ŝ1:T = argmaxs1:T Σt [ log bst(xt) + log a(st−1, st) ]  subject to  every run of state s lasting ≥ ds,
(3)

computed per half with a chain-structured recursion in O(K) per frame for K = 175 sub-states (about 3 s per half). A test checks it against dense Viterbi.

3.3  Training labels and weights

The challenge states that a loose or contested ball stays with the last team and a change needs controlled possession. The external labels flip on every touch, so they teach a model to split too often. We relabel: an alive run of team k shorter than 50 frames (2 s, fixed from the wording, not tuned) that directly follows a run of the other team is given to that team. Runs after a stoppage are kept. Because spellF1 counts possessions rather than frames, each training frame in a run of n frames is weighted by

w(n) = clip( (200 / n)1/2, 0.25, 4 ).
(4)

The team model (50 features: soft ball-to-team proximity over windows from one frame to 12 s, sustained control, touch decays, team width) is a logistic regression without intercept on home-minus-away features, trained from both viewpoints, so swapping the teams gives exactly 1 − phome.

3.4  M8: a gated stoppage model

On our match 98.6% of long, slow ball gaps are already labelled dead, so the stoppages an earlier model (M7) missed were short ones with the ball in view: throw-ins, fouls, quick restarts. M8 replaces the dead model with two LightGBM [12] experts, chosen by whether the ball is seen at frame t:

pdead(t) = fvis(xt)  if the ball is seen,   fgap(x̃t)  otherwise,
(5)

where x̃ drops every feature that reads the ball. New features are player speed as within-match quantiles (mean, 90th percentile, share walking) over 0.04, 3 and 10 s, a sustained slow-down, the ball standing still for 1.5 s, distance to each line, and gap facts (length, time into it, time left, where the ball was last and next seen). Features that only identify the data source, such as the number of tracked players, are removed after an adversarial check separated every external match from ours on them alone. Each expert has 300 trees, deterministic settings, and a class weight of 1.5 on dead rows that is undone at prediction time.

4  Data and Validation Protocol

Training data. No label of the provided match is used. All labels come from nine matches of other games (Table 1).

Degradation. External balls are much cleaner than ours, so every match is used under several conditions: full (unchanged); deadmiss (78% of stoppages and 13% of contacts hidden); n10dm and n16dm (deadmiss plus 1.0 / 1.6 m of player noise drifting over 4 s); n13corrupt (our missing-ball mask transplanted, plus 1.3 m noise); and, for M8, three simulator draws sim0–sim2. The simulator hides each true stoppage (always if ≥ 10 s, otherwise with probability 0.29) from 2.8 s after the whistle to 3.4 s before the restart, adds label-independent dropouts (1.3 per minute), and low-passes the ball so fewer touches are credited. Its parameters are fitted to our match's label-free statistics: missing share, gap lengths, gaps per minute, in-gap player speed and credited touches per minute.

Protocol. Models are refit out of fold, so a match is never scored by a model that saw it. Scoring calls the organisers' possession_metrics through our real decoder. For M8 the keep rule was written before any run: mean gain ≥ 0.02 and above its standard error, at least 7 of 9 matches better, none worse by more than 0.03, and clean-ball spellF1 down by at most 0.01. The public five-minute window (17 possessions, noise about ±0.28) was used only as a correctness check.

Decoder behaviour. When a stoppage is found, its boundaries have a median error of 0 frames. Direct H↔A switches show no bias but an interquartile spread of about ±18 frames, and only 33–62% of true switches have a predicted switch within 2 s. Moving switches to the new owner's first touch changes nothing, so the loss is in switches the model misses, not in their timing.

8  Limitations

  • The harness is a proxy. A classifier still tells the simulator from our match (AUC 0.83–0.89, against 0.91 for the older conditions), and touches near a player are three times rarer on our match than on any external match.
  • Interceptions are the main loss. Only a third to two thirds of direct switches are found within 2 s. Ball detection on video, validated on labelled external video, is the next step.
  • Smart Tagging cannot be validated directly; we simulate it from external truth, but its timing error on our match is unknown.
  • Label conventions differ. IDSSE splits possession about twice as finely as the target definition, and 5 of its 7 matches share a home team.
  • Stoppage durations. Our match's predicted stoppages are shorter than in any external match under both M7 and M8, and its dead share (33.6%) is below typical league values.

9  Reproducibility and Disclosure

Python 3.12/3.13 with pinned numpy 2.3.2, pandas 2.3.2, scipy 1.16.2 and LightGBM 4.6.0. From a fresh clone and the data bundle:

pip install -r requirements.txt
export MTAH_DATA_DIR=/path/to/data/bundle
python -m rondo.signals.export --out out/evidence.npz
python -m rondo.run --evidence out/evidence.npz --out "out/The Misfits.csv"

This reproduces the submitted file byte for byte (SHA-256 744dd93f207172cc…); --model m7 reproduces the previous submission (cf22e581…). Training commands for M7 and M8 and the decoder experiments are in the repository; the tests need no match data.

Disclosure. No hand labels and no human-produced data about the provided match are used; its full ground truth is never read, and the public window is a correctness check only. Label-free quantities taken from its tracking are the missing-ball mask, the median player speed, and the simulator's fitting statistics. Smart Tagging is used as automatic input, as the FAQ allows. Only aggregate numbers from our own scripts were shared with an AI assistant; no match data was uploaded to any AI service. No match data is in the repository; the committed model files are fitted on external data only.
